# 32. The Empty Fort

*Part VI: Stratagems for Desperate Situations*

1. The gates of the city stood open, the streets were swept, and a man sat on the wall with his lute. What turned the enemy was not the garrison. It was the absence of one.

2. An empty fort cannot be dressed. A pretended garrison is found out; an open gate is not.

3. In a drawdown the account has nothing to show. Show nothing: fewer trades, smaller, slower.

4. The position not taken cannot lose. Its maximum loss is known to the cent, and it needs no stop.

5. Cash is not a failure of nerve. It is the wall the next trade stands behind.

6. When the options fort is empty — no strike, no bid, no open interest — it is not garrisoned with a market order.

7. The same thesis can hold another wall: a company priced below its net cash is a fort whose gates open from the inside.

8. A buyer of cheap convexity wants the cheapest expression of the outcome, and the cheapest is not always an option.

9. The empty fort is still a position, and it needs a time stop: held until the enemy passes, not forever.

10. Doing nothing, done correctly, is the only thing being done.

## Commentary

The empty fort is not a trick that works because the enemy is stupid. It works because the fort is genuinely empty, and the open gate is the truth. A drawdown is the same. When the account has no edge that meets the standard, the honest posture is no position. A small position taken to look busy is the pretended garrison, and it is found out at the next open.

No position is the cleanest arithmetic in the book. Maximum loss is zero, no stop is needed, and time decay has nothing to charge. Every cost the buyer carries begins with holding. To stand aside is not to forgo the game. It is to keep the capital that the next dated catalyst will require.

Sometimes the fort is not empty but untenable. There is no chain, no bid, no strike worth the spread, and the options road has no wall to hold. Then the same judgement can be expressed in another instrument. A company trading below its net cash is a fort whose gate opens from within, because the buyer's price already contains the cash. Jamie Mai, as described in Jack Schwager's Hedge Fund Market Wizards, is a buyer of cheap convexity who looks for the cheapest instrument that captures the outcome; when options do not serve, the stock itself can. Lewis's The Big Short tells the same practice as patience rather than a signal.
