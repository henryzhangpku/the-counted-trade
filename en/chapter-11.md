# 11. Sacrifice the Plum for the Peach

*Part II: Stratagems for Confronting the Enemy*

1. A worm came to the peach root, and the plum tree beside it took the bite and stiffened in its place. The peach lived.

2. The far strike is the plum. Sell it, and the near strike is bought cheaper; the debit that remains is the whole risk.

3. Buy the 100 call, sell the 110 call, pay 3. The ticket states the count before the market moves: maximum loss 300, maximum gain 700, breakeven 103.

4. Breakeven win rate is the debit divided by the width of the spread. Three in ten at 3; two and a half at 2.50.

5. At 2.50 the trade is 3:1, with 250 at risk and 750 to gain. The arithmetic does not care about the story.

6. The plum sold is the tail above 110, the part of the move the buyer has agreed not to be paid for.

7. A long call at ten-to-one keeps the whole tail and needs one win in eleven. The spread needs three in ten, and a move to 103.

8. Know which tree you are in. If the thesis is a doubling, the spread caps it; if the thesis is a move to 108, the single leg is a donation.

9. The short leg is a loan against conviction, repaid out of the move above the short strike.

10. A spread is not a smaller long. It is a different wager on the same direction: the tail sold, the odds moved.

## Commentary

The plum is the strike sold, and the sacrifice is exact. A ten-wide spread bought for 3 has a maximum loss of 300, a maximum gain of 700 and a breakeven at 103. The breakeven win rate is the debit divided by the width, 3 divided by 10, or 30 per cent. Pay 2.50 instead and the same spread risks 250 to make 750, a ratio of 3:1, and the breakeven win rate falls to 25 per cent.

The single long call bought at ten-to-one has a different count. Risk one to make ten, and one win in eleven is enough to break even, a little over 9 per cent. That looks easier than 30 per cent until the event is named: the ten-to-one ticket needs a near-doubling, and the 3:1 spread needs a move to 103. The spread has traded the tenfold tail for a breakeven price a three-dollar move can reach. Which is better depends on what the thesis actually predicts, not on which payoff is prettier.

This is the arithmetic behind Mai's practice of risking one to make ten, as Schwager records it in Hedge Fund Market Wizards and Lewis in The Big Short. Most of those bets lose; the few that pay carry the book. The buyer who keeps the tenfold tail accepts many losses; the one who sells it accepts that the doubling will be paid to someone else. Mixing the two — a spread sized like a lottery ticket, a lottery ticket held like a spread — is not coherent.
