# 4. Wait at Leisure for the Exhausted Enemy

*Part I: Stratagems for Winning*

1. The general does not chase. He holds the pass, keeps his men in the shade, and lets the enemy's march do half the work.

2. For the buyer of options the march is time, and it does not tire the enemy. It tires the buyer. Every day held, the debit is eaten by an opponent who never sleeps and never asks for terms.

3. Therefore patience has a deadline. The sentence is: I exit on this date if the thesis has not confirmed, regardless of price.

4. Regardless of price is the whole of it. A stop that moves with hope is not a stop. It is a decision postponed at a daily cost.

5. The buyer either buys enough time or sets a tight stop. Short time and a loose stop is the one combination with no defence: the thesis is given no room to arrive, and the loss is given all the room in the world.

6. Mai's answer to time, as he described it to Schwager, was to buy years rather than weeks, so that the thesis had the months it needed and the time stop could be set by the catalyst rather than by impatience.

7. The date is not chosen for comfort. It is the day after the last event that could confirm the thesis, or the day the money must go elsewhere, whichever comes first.

8. Waiting at leisure is not waiting without a plan. The general in the shade has already chosen the pass, the day, and the order in which his men will stand.

9. The buyer's exhausted enemy is not volatility. It is the passage of days with no catalyst, and no amount of watching makes those days cheaper.

10. The buyer who cannot name the date of his own retreat has not chosen patience. He has chosen to be patient until the money is gone.

## Commentary

A long option is a rented position, and the rent is charged daily whether or not the thesis is progressing. It is small enough to ignore on any single morning and large enough to matter over a quarter. That is why the time stop is the buyer's first defence: it turns open-ended hope into a dated question.

The failures are symmetrical. A one-year thesis in a thirty-day option is not early; it is the wrong instrument. A thirty-day thesis with a loose stop is not patient; it is rent paid on a conclusion already reached. Buy the time the thesis needs, or set the stop the time allows. Never short time and a loose stop.

Mai's version was not a tolerance for waiting but an insistence that the option's life match the event's: if the thesis waited on a decision that might take a year, the option had to survive a year, and the exit date belonged to the decision, not to the trader's discomfort. Patience with a date on it is a plan. Patience without one is the rent.
