# 25. Replace the Beams With Rotten Timbers

*Part V: Stratagems for Gaining Ground*

1. A hall is raised beam by beam. Swap the beams for rotten timber, paint them true, and the roof still sits — until the first snow.

2. A backtest is such a hall. Its beams are the trades it counts; the load it must bear is the trade actually placed.

3. The substitution is quiet. A hit rate is put where a win rate belongs: that a stock doubled within a quarter is a fact about the stock, not the record of a stopped trade.

4. A hit rate counts arrivals. A trade counts exits — the premium paid, the date of the stop, the price at which the spread can be sold.

5. So each expression must be tested on its own timber: the stock, the long call, the debit spread, the spread at the price the buyer can actually pay.

6. The second rotten beam is a rule that was never a rule. "Buy cheap convexity when the stock is quiet" sounds like a method. It is one man's judgement, compressed into a sentence by someone else.

7. Jamie Mai, as he describes his practice in Schwager's *Hedge Fund Market Wizards* and as Lewis tells it in *The Big Short*, was a buyer of cheap convexity. The quiet was his entry condition. The event was his edge.

8. Remove the event and the screen is a roof with nothing beneath it. A screen cannot borrow an eye.

9. When the beam is judgement, no filter cut from it will carry the same load.

## Commentary

The first substitution is arithmetic. A study counted how often a stock doubled within sixty-three sessions and found it happened 12.6% of the time among crowded-short picks, against 1.6% for random picks. That is a hit rate at the stock level. It says nothing about the win rate of a stopped option trade on the same names: the premium, the time stop and the exit price all sit between the two numbers. Present the first as the second and the house stands on rotten timber.

The second substitution is harder to see because it wears the clothes of a rule. A pre-registered test took Mai's entry condition — a stock whose realized volatility sat at a three-year low — and bought twelve-month calls struck 30% out of the money, 2020 to 2025, 580 trades. At a realistic price the test returned about $0.58 per $1 of premium; about 88% expired worthless; the calls did no better than the same calls on ordinary stocks.

What the test removed was the beam that carried the roof. Mai's quiet stock was a place to look, not a reason to buy. The reason was an event he had judged — a court, a regulator, a solvency question — and a screen has no eye for that. Each expression needs its own test. When the beam is judgement, the test can only tell you that the timber you substituted was rotten.
