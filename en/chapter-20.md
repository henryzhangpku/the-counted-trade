# 20. Fish in Troubled Waters

*Part IV: Stratagems for Confused Situations*

1. The fisherman who cannot see the bottom stirs the mud. The fish rise gasping, and the net does the rest.

2. In a high-volatility market the water is already muddy. Protection and lottery tickets are bought in the same hour, and both are marked up.

3. Premium is dearest when buying feels most urgent. The crowd is not paying for information; it is paying for its own alarm.

4. To reach for a long single leg in that water is to buy the alarm at its peak. The position carries its largest vega at the moment it is least needed.

5. Fish with the spread. The strike that is sold returns part of the inflated premium, and the vega of the position is a difference between two vegas, not one.

6. A debit spread in muddy water is a net with a lid. Less is paid, less has to be true, and the same move still pays.

7. The far bank is the seller's ground: naked puts, ratios, income promised in a drawdown. Even there, only defined risk. The buyer's book has no business crossing.

8. Muddy water is not the buyer's ally. It is his price, and he beats it only by paying less for it than the crowd.

## Commentary

The stratagem has the fisherman make the mud himself. The market needs no help. A volatility shock muddies the water for everyone, and the fear that lifts implied volatility lifts the price of every structure built on it. Demand for protection and demand for lottery tickets arrive together. The buyer who acts on urgency pays the top of both.

The buyer's error here is structural rather than directional. A long single leg carries the full vega of the chain's fear. A debit spread carries the difference between two vegas, and the strike that is sold hands back part of the inflated premium. The view can be identical; the price of admission is not. In muddy water the spread is the cheaper way to hold the same thesis, which is the whole of the argument and none of the forecast.

The far bank is the seller's book. A buyer who crosses over becomes a seller without a seller's collateral or temperament, and the mud is deepest exactly where the income looks easiest. The buyer's discipline is narrower: keep the risk defined, keep the position small enough that the mud cannot swallow it, and let the water clear before judging the trade.
