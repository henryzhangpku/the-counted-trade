# 6. Clamour in the East, Strike in the West

*Part I: Stratagems for Winning*

1. Drums in the east, the army crosses in the west. The noise is not a lie about the war. It is a lie about the place.

2. The edge at one level is not the edge at another. A finding about stocks remains a finding about stocks until it has been tested in the instrument that will carry it.

3. The count is plain. Crowded-short picks doubled 12.6% of the time; random picks, 1.6%. That is a stock-level result, and a strong one.

4. The same picks, expressed as call spreads, were tradeable in only 10 of 87 cases. Of those ten, one won. The pilot's mean was −62%.

5. The noise in the stock did not reach the options battlefield. The direction was right and the structure still lost, because the move arrived late, or small, or already paid for.

6. Therefore, before the order, ask where the edge actually lands. If the claim is about the price of the stock, it belongs in the stock, or in a structure that can survive the path.

7. Strike where the edge lands. Sometimes the option is the cheap expression. Sometimes it is the shares, and the option only adds a clock the thesis does not need.

8. The cheapest expression of a business priced below the cash on its books may be the shares, not the calls. Mai's habit, as he described it, was to buy the cheapest instrument that captured the outcome.

9. A feint works only if the enemy believes it. A backtest in one instrument, presented as a strategy in another, is a feint played on yourself.

10. The drums are cheap. The crossing is what costs. Count the crossing.

## Commentary

The two numbers belong together, and reading either alone is the error. At the stock level the signal was real: 12.6% against 1.6% is a wide gap, and it survives the objection that the picks were merely volatile. The trap is the next step, where the signal is assumed to travel into another instrument with its edge intact.

It did not travel. Of 87 cases where a call spread could express the idea, only 10 were tradeable at all, and of those ten, one won. A pilot mean of −62% is not a weak version of a strong stock result. It is a different trade wearing the same name. The option carried a clock, a spread, and an implied volatility that repriced the very move the signal predicted; the stock signal was measured without any of them.

So the question is not whether the edge is real but where it lands. Sometimes it lands in the option, when the mispricing is in the chain. Sometimes the chain is expensive or absent, and the shares carry the thesis better. In *The Big Short*, Lewis describes Mai buying the cheapest thing that expressed a view. A company trading below its net cash is the plainest case: the shares are the expression, and no option is needed.
