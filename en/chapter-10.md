# 10. Hide a Dagger Behind a Smile

*Part II: Stratagems for Confronting the Enemy*

1. The host smiles, fills the cup, and speaks of old friendship. The blade is under the sleeve, and the sleeve is long.

2. The far weekly smiles in the same way. A few cents on the screen buy a fortune at the far strike, and the fortune is drawn where the curve almost never goes.

3. The smile is the price. The dagger is the probability.

4. Cheapness is not a gift. It is a quote for a thing that almost never happens, made by people who have counted.

5. Count the smile: the bid-ask paid on entry and again on exit, and the theta that charges rent for every day the far strike stays far.

6. The far strike must be crossed twice, once by the stock and once by the clock. The first is unlikely. The second is certain.

7. Weekly theta is not a slope. It is a cliff, and the last days are its edge.

8. A call whose delta is two in a hundred has, by the market's own rough reckoning, about one chance in fifty of finishing in the money, and needs more than that to pay.

9. Asymmetry is a ratio of payoff to cost, not a feeling about the payoff. A ticket priced at the whole expectancy is not asymmetric.

10. The buyer who wants the tail must buy time with it, or a nearer strike, or nothing. The dagger is in his own hand, and he gives it away at the fill.

## Commentary

The far out-of-the-money weekly is the purest picture of asymmetry on the screen: a few cents against a strike that would pay many times the cost. The picture omits two charges. The first is the spread, paid on the way in and on the way out. The second is theta, which at the far strike is not a slope but a cliff, because the last days of a weekly carry almost all of the remaining extrinsic value.

The volatility smile is the honest name of the cheapness. The surface is not flat: a far strike carries its own implied volatility, and the option is cheap in dollars because the probability is small, not because its volatility is cheap. The buyer is paying for the picture, and the picture is what the seller is selling.

Take a call with a delta of 0.02. The market's rough odds of finishing in the money are about one in fifty; to make money the stock must finish well beyond the strike, not at it. Multiply that by the spread and by the days of decay, and the expectancy is poor before any view about direction is formed.

None of this makes the far strike wrong. It makes it a price. A buyer who wants that payoff must pay for time as well as distance, or move the strike closer, or decline the trade. The dagger is not hidden from him. It is in his hand from the moment of the fill.
