# 19. Remove the Firewood From Under the Pot

*Part IV: Stratagems for Confused Situations*

1. To still a boiling pot, no one ladles the water. A man draws the firewood from beneath it, and the boil dies of itself.

2. A loss is the boiling; the size that feeds it is the firewood. To argue with the price after entry is to ladle, and the pot boils faster for it.

3. Before the first order, write the worst case in one sentence: *my worst case is three hundred dollars, because the debit is three.*

4. For a long option the debit is the whole risk. For a debit spread it is the width less the credit. For stock it is the distance to the stop, times the shares.

5. Divide the risk budget by the worst case of one unit, and round down. What remains is not idle money; it is the firewood left outside the wall.

6. If one contract is over the budget, take a narrower spread, a smaller underlying, or no trade. Never round up to one.

7. Risk one to make ten is a constraint before it is a hope: the one must be small enough to lose ten times in a row and still sit down to count the eleventh.

8. In his interview in Jack Schwager's *Hedge Fund Market Wizards*, Jamie Mai described a book of such bets, most of them expected to fail, the few that paid carrying all the rest. The arithmetic begins with the one, not the ten.

9. A three-dollar spread wins seven and loses three. That is the pot. How many pots the account can feed at once is the firewood.

10. The pot does not know why it cools. The market owes no man a reason, only the bill.

## Commentary

The stratagem strikes at the cause of the boil, not at the boil. In a trade the visible thing is the loss on the screen; the cause is the size that made the loss matter. A trader can spend an afternoon reasoning with a losing spread — the thesis is intact, the date is close, the chart sits on support — and every argument is a ladle. The firewood is untouched underneath: too many contracts for the worst case.

The arithmetic is easy, which is why it is so often skipped. A 100 call bought and a 110 call sold for a $3 debit: the width is $10, the debit $3, so the maximum loss is $300 and the maximum gain $700. If the risk budget is $1,000, three spreads cost $900 and the fourth costs $1,200. It is $200 over, so the count is three. Rounding up to four does not make the risk $1,000. It makes the plan a fiction.

Mai's version of risk one to make ten is often quoted as though the ten were the point. In Schwager's interview the one comes first, and it is small. The bets are expected to fail, and a book has to survive a run of failures to collect the one that does not. Ten bets at a tenth each, all lost, is the whole book. That is not convexity; it is ten chances to be finished before the thesis arrives.
