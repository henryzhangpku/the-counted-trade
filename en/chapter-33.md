# 33. Let the Enemy's Spy Sow Discord

*Part VI: Stratagems for Desperate Situations*

1. A letter left on the desk for the spy to steal has done its work before it is read; the hand that kills is the enemy's own.

2. The option chain is the enemy's spy in your camp, reporting daily what others fear: implied move, skew, term structure, the price of borrow.

3. An intercepted report is useful twice: for what it says, and for why someone wished it said.

4. The implied move is a price, not a prophecy. It is what the crowd paid to insure itself against its own fear.

5. Skew is the spy's accent. It tells you which tail he is afraid of.

6. Term structure is the spy's calendar: next week's fear and next year's fear are priced apart.

7. Stale data is a double agent. It answers with yesterday's map and charges today's price.

8. A good map is worth paying for, and the cost of the map belongs inside the trade.

9. The deepest secret is the shape: a smooth curve laid over an event that can only go two ways.

10. Read where the spy's map is wrong, and let the enemy's intelligence point your road.

## Commentary

The stratagem is not bribery. It is making the other side's own judgement do your work. The chain reports a great deal: open interest, volume, implied volatility, the slope of skew, the spread between near and far months, the difficulty of borrowing. None of it is advice. It is a record of what other people paid to be afraid of, and its use is to show where that fear is priced too high or too low.

The implied move is the plainest report. The at-the-money straddle divided by the stock price is what the market has set for one event; set against the last eight to twelve realised moves, it gives a ratio. The ratio is arithmetic. The direction is not. A stale map — old filings, old share counts, an old borrow fee — is worse than no map, because it is charged at today's price.

The shape is the deeper reading. When an outcome is binary — a court ruling, a regulator's decision, a solvency question — the stock has two futures, but option prices are built on one smooth curve. The curve smears two futures into a continuum, and the ends are mispriced. That is the gap Jamie Mai describes in his interview in Jack Schwager's Hedge Fund Market Wizards: a buyer of cheap convexity looking at a two-humped world priced as one hump. The spy's map is wrong at the edges, and that is where it is read.
