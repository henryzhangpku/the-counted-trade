# Before the Count

A stratagem is a trick played on an enemy. The Thirty-Six Stratagems do not pretend otherwise: show weakness where you are strong, offer a bait worth taking, let the enemy's own certainty do the work. A stratagem succeeds less because the enemy is foolish than because he sees what he expects to see.

In trading, the enemy most often tricked is yourself.

This is the buyer's book. *The Art of Options War* reads the seller of the defined-risk credit spread, paid to hold a line. *The Counted Trade* stands on the other side of that trade. The buyer pays a debit, and the debit is the whole risk; his upside is the asymmetry he paid for. Time is his enemy, volatility his price, and a catalyst his only ally. His art is choosing which mispricing, timing which dated event, paying the right price, and committing correctly: conviction in the rules, a staged ladder, and a size small enough that one loss is trivial.

Six sets of six. Each chapter turns one stratagem's image to the buyer's problem. Before any trade, five sentences must each be sayable in one sentence: the edge, the catalyst, the max loss, the time stop, the breakeven win rate. A trade that cannot say all five is a hope trade.

A second voice runs beside them: Jamie Mai, a buyer of cheap convexity, as told in Jack Schwager's *Hedge Fund Market Wizards* and Michael Lewis's *The Big Short*. The book also records where a mechanical copy of his method failed. The quiet was only his entry condition; the event was his edge. A screen cannot borrow his eye.

This book is education, not advice. It contains no personal recommendations, no signals and no promises. Options can lose money faster than stock. A long option can lose its entire premium, and a debit spread can lose its whole debit; the debit can still be more than you can afford. Nothing here is a performance claim.

Read one chapter a day. Then read them again in the sets' order, for the order is the argument: count before the battle, read the other side's price, pass the funnel, hold the max loss in chaos, test without fooling yourself, and know when to leave.

# 1. Cross the Sea Under Camouflage

*Part I: Stratagems for Winning*

1. The emperor feared the sea, so a general built him a house upon the water: painted walls, a threshold, a floor that did not move. He crossed the sea believing he sat on land.

2. So the hope trade enters the account: dressed as conviction, furnished like a plan, and no one aboard asks what water runs beneath the floor.

3. The camouflage is not aimed at the enemy. It is aimed at the passenger. The enemy is the price; the passenger is the buyer.

4. Therefore, before the order, say the five sentences aloud: the edge, the catalyst, the max loss, the time stop, the breakeven win rate.

5. Say them where another can hear, or write them where the later self will read. A sentence that cannot be said is a wall painted on water.

6. The edge is one sentence: what is mispriced, why that is measurable, and what must happen after costs. "I think it goes up" is a wish with a ticket attached.

7. The max loss is the debit, known before the entry and not discovered after it. If the number cannot be spoken, the size was chosen by appetite.

8. Conviction belongs to the rules and to the thesis. It has no business in the size. A size that grows with feeling is the sea showing through the paint.

9. The buyer who cannot say all five is not a buyer of asymmetry. He is a passenger who has paid the fare and believes he is standing on a floor.

10. The sea does not care what the house looks like. At the far shore it asks only whether the floor was real.

## Commentary

The old story has an emperor who would not board a ship, and a general who built him a room on one. The room was real enough to walk in and false in everything that mattered. The hope trade is built to the same plan: a thesis with a grain of truth, a chart that agrees with it, a size that feels like commitment, and no floor under any of it.

The test is cheap, and it is verbal. Five sentences before the order: what is mispriced; which dated event reprices it; what the debit is; on what date the trade is abandoned if the thesis has not confirmed; what win rate the price demands. These are not a ritual. Each one closes a door through which the hope trade would otherwise walk, and the fourth is the one most often left painted shut, because a time stop costs nothing to promise and something to keep.

Conviction, in this book, is a property of rules and thesis, never of size. A trader may be entirely convinced and still take one contract, because the conviction sits in the plan, and the plan already carries the risk. When conviction moves into the size, the camouflage has worked. The passenger is at sea, and believes he is on a floor.

# 2. Besiege Wei to Rescue Zhao

*Part I: Stratagems for Winning*

1. When Wei besieged Handan, the relief did not ride to Handan. It rode to Daliang, the enemy's own capital, and the siege broke behind it.

2. The price is Handan: visible, loud, and not where the trade is won. The mispricing is Daliang: the place the price must return to.

3. Therefore do not attack the price. Attack the reason it is wrong.

4. An edge is one sentence with three parts: I expect this mispricing, because of this measurable reason, and after costs this structure wins if this condition holds.

5. "I think it goes up" is not an edge. It is a direction with a feeling attached, and feelings carry no mechanism.

6. The measurable reason may be a spread, a filing, a dated decision, a forced seller. What cannot be measured is a hope with a chart.

7. After costs is not an afterthought. Commission, spread and decay are the road the army must march, and an edge that dies on the road was never an edge.

8. Wins if this condition holds: the structure must pay when that condition is met, not when every condition is met.

9. Mai's form of the siege: where an outcome is binary, a ruling or a regulator or a solvency question, the stock has two futures and the chain prices one smooth curve. Besiege the curve, and the direction rescues itself.

10. The general who relieves a siege need not reach the besieged. He need only make the besieger turn. The buyer need only make the price turn, and he needs a reason it must.

## Commentary

The sentence in the fourth aphorism is an audit, not a slogan. Read it back and each clause can fail. What is the mispricing, exactly? Where is the measurement, and can it be checked without the trade? After costs, does the structure still pay, or does the edge live only before commission and spread? And if the condition does not hold, is the structure simply wrong, with no story left to save it?

Most trades offered as ideas fail at the first clause. "It goes up" names a direction and calls it a mispricing. A direction is a forecast; a mispricing is a statement about a price being wrong for a nameable reason. The reason is what lets a buyer survive being early, because it tells him what to check and when.

Jamie Mai's version, as he described it to Schwager in *Hedge Fund Market Wizards* and as Lewis tells it in *The Big Short*, attacked the shape of the pricing rather than the direction of the stock. Where a company's future depended on a single decision, the option chain still priced a smooth spread of outcomes. Buying the cheap part of that curve did not require knowing which future would arrive. That is the siege: the pricing is the wall, and the stock's direction rescues itself.

# 3. Kill With a Borrowed Knife

*Part I: Stratagems for Winning*

1. A knife is borrowed, not forged. The hand supplies the direction; another man's weight supplies the blow. The borrower's whole art is knowing where the blade will pass.

2. The buyer's knife is dated. An event on a day, a mechanism that reprices, and a chain that has not yet charged for either.

3. Therefore the sentence is: this event, on this date, should reprice the stock, because of this mechanism. A catalyst without a date is a rumour. A date without a mechanism is a coin.

4. Earnings, a rate decision, a price report, a drug decision, a court date, an index rebalance, an expiration. The calendar is short, and the knife must stand on it.

5. Every candidate goes on one calendar of forty-five days. What falls outside it is not a catalyst for this trade. It is another trade, entered early.

6. The sharpest knife is a binary one: a ruling or a regulator's decision, where the stock has two futures and the chain prices one curve.

7. In that case the buyer need not know which future arrives. He needs the two futures to be priced as one, and the knife does the rest.

8. A borrowed knife has two edges. If the date passes and the price has not moved, the event was the wrong knife, and the trade is wrong even if the story is still good.

9. Therefore: no dated mechanism, no catalyst. No catalyst, no trade. The buyer pays for time, and time without an event is rent paid to nobody.

10. The wise buyer does not swing hardest. He waits until the enemy's own weight is already moving, and holds the blade where it must pass.

## Commentary

The calendar does the discipline. Forty-five days holds a company's next report, a macro date and one legal hearing; it is short enough that the buyer is not paying for a year of hope. Every candidate goes on that single sheet, the funnel's first gate: a thesis whose event is not on it is not ready, however good the story.

The events divide into four families. Company events: earnings, guidance, an investor day, a drug decision. Macro events: the rate decision, the inflation print, the employment report. Legal and regulatory events: a ruling, an approval, an investigation. Mechanical events: an index rebalance, a large expiration, a lockup. Each has a date and each has a mechanism by which it reaches the price. Name the mechanism or the date is only a day on which something might happen.

The binary knife is sharpest because the mispricing is structural. When the outcome is yes or no, the stock has two futures, but the chain quotes a continuum. As Mai put it in Schwager's *Hedge Fund Market Wizards*, the price of that continuum can be wrong in a way that needs no forecast. The buyer supplies the date and the judgement that the event is real; the event supplies the force.

# 4. Wait at Leisure for the Exhausted Enemy

*Part I: Stratagems for Winning*

1. The general does not chase. He holds the pass, keeps his men in the shade, and lets the enemy's march do half the work.

2. For the buyer of options the march is time, and it does not tire the enemy. It tires the buyer. Every day held, the debit is eaten by an opponent who never sleeps and never asks for terms.

3. Therefore patience has a deadline. The sentence is: I exit on this date if the thesis has not confirmed, regardless of price.

4. Regardless of price is the whole of it. A stop that moves with hope is not a stop. It is a decision postponed at a daily cost.

5. The buyer either buys enough time or sets a tight stop. Short time and a loose stop is the one combination with no defence: the thesis is given no room to arrive, and the loss is given all the room in the world.

6. Mai's answer to time, as he described it to Schwager, was to buy years rather than weeks, so that the thesis had the months it needed and the time stop could be set by the catalyst rather than by impatience.

7. The date is not chosen for comfort. It is the day after the last event that could confirm the thesis, or the day the money must go elsewhere, whichever comes first.

8. Waiting at leisure is not waiting without a plan. The general in the shade has already chosen the pass, the day, and the order in which his men will stand.

9. The buyer's exhausted enemy is not volatility. It is the passage of days with no catalyst, and no amount of watching makes those days cheaper.

10. The buyer who cannot name the date of his own retreat has not chosen patience. He has chosen to be patient until the money is gone.

## Commentary

A long option is a rented position, and the rent is charged daily whether or not the thesis is progressing. It is small enough to ignore on any single morning and large enough to matter over a quarter. That is why the time stop is the buyer's first defence: it turns open-ended hope into a dated question.

The failures are symmetrical. A one-year thesis in a thirty-day option is not early; it is the wrong instrument. A thirty-day thesis with a loose stop is not patient; it is rent paid on a conclusion already reached. Buy the time the thesis needs, or set the stop the time allows. Never short time and a loose stop.

Mai's version was not a tolerance for waiting but an insistence that the option's life match the event's: if the thesis waited on a decision that might take a year, the option had to survive a year, and the exit date belonged to the decision, not to the trader's discomfort. Patience with a date on it is a plan. Patience without one is the rent.

# 5. Loot a Burning House

*Part I: Stratagems for Winning*

1. The house is already burning. The looter does not light it. He stands at the door and takes what the fire throws out.

2. The fire here is fear of a dated event, and fear has a price. The implied move is what the crowd pays to be excused from the room.

3. Measure the fire first. Divide the at-the-money straddle by the share price; that is the move the chain expects.

4. Set it against the last eight to twelve actual moves after the same event. If implied is above 1.3 times that history, and the implied volatility rank is above 70, the fire is overpriced, and the buyer is the one being looted.

5. If implied is below 0.8 times the history, and the rank is below 30, the fire is underpriced. The event is being sold cheap, and the buyer pays less than the move has been worth.

6. Between those bands there is no trade. A fire measured at the market's own estimate is not a fire. It is a price.

7. Loot only the fire you have measured. An event that feels frightening is not the same as one cheaply priced, and the feeling is usually the thing being charged for.

8. There is a second fire, and it is not in the chain. Bad news can push a price below what the business can survive at. The crowd runs; the buyer stands where it ran from.

9. There the work is not volatility but solvency: what the company owes, when it must pay, and whether the price already assumes the worst.

10. Two fires, two measures. If you cannot say which one you are looting, you are standing in the third, the one you lit yourself.

## Commentary

The arithmetic of the first fire is simple enough for the back of a card: divide the at-the-money straddle by the share price, and the quotient is the move the chain expects. Compare it with the last eight to twelve moves the stock actually made after the same event. A stock whose recent reports barely moved it is being priced for something it has not recently done.

The ratios turn the comparison into a rule. Above 1.3 times history, with implied volatility high in its range, the buyer pays for fear the history does not support. Below 0.8 times, with volatility near its lows, the event is sold too cheaply, and the asymmetry is on the shelf. Between the bands the price is fair, and a fair price is not a trade, however strong the opinion.

The second fire is a different measurement. It is not the size of a move but the floor under the business: whether the bad news has taken the price below what the assets and cash can bear. In *The Big Short*, Lewis describes Mai and his partner looking for exactly this, businesses the crowd had sold past what they could be worth. No volatility screen finds that. It is read in the filings.

# 6. Clamour in the East, Strike in the West

*Part I: Stratagems for Winning*

1. Drums in the east, the army crosses in the west. The noise is not a lie about the war. It is a lie about the place.

2. The edge at one level is not the edge at another. A finding about stocks remains a finding about stocks until it has been tested in the instrument that will carry it.

3. The count is plain. Crowded-short picks doubled 12.6% of the time; random picks, 1.6%. That is a stock-level result, and a strong one.

4. The same picks, expressed as call spreads, were tradeable in only 10 of 87 cases. Of those ten, one won. The pilot's mean was −62%.

5. The noise in the stock did not reach the options battlefield. The direction was right and the structure still lost, because the move arrived late, or small, or already paid for.

6. Therefore, before the order, ask where the edge actually lands. If the claim is about the price of the stock, it belongs in the stock, or in a structure that can survive the path.

7. Strike where the edge lands. Sometimes the option is the cheap expression. Sometimes it is the shares, and the option only adds a clock the thesis does not need.

8. The cheapest expression of a business priced below the cash on its books may be the shares, not the calls. Mai's habit, as he described it, was to buy the cheapest instrument that captured the outcome.

9. A feint works only if the enemy believes it. A backtest in one instrument, presented as a strategy in another, is a feint played on yourself.

10. The drums are cheap. The crossing is what costs. Count the crossing.

## Commentary

The two numbers belong together, and reading either alone is the error. At the stock level the signal was real: 12.6% against 1.6% is a wide gap, and it survives the objection that the picks were merely volatile. The trap is the next step, where the signal is assumed to travel into another instrument with its edge intact.

It did not travel. Of 87 cases where a call spread could express the idea, only 10 were tradeable at all, and of those ten, one won. A pilot mean of −62% is not a weak version of a strong stock result. It is a different trade wearing the same name. The option carried a clock, a spread, and an implied volatility that repriced the very move the signal predicted; the stock signal was measured without any of them.

So the question is not whether the edge is real but where it lands. Sometimes it lands in the option, when the mispricing is in the chain. Sometimes the chain is expensive or absent, and the shares carry the thesis better. In *The Big Short*, Lewis describes Mai buying the cheapest thing that expressed a view. A company trading below its net cash is the plainest case: the shares are the expression, and no option is needed.

# 7. Create Something From Nothing

*Part II: Stratagems for Confronting the Enemy*

1. Straw men went over the wall at night. The besiegers loosed at them until the quivers were empty, and by dawn the garrison had arrows. The next night the real men came down.

2. A backtest works the same way. Turn a filter, move a threshold, and a dead rule stands up in the data wearing the shape of an edge.

3. Nothing was found. Something was made. The curve that only rises at the tenth variation is a straw man at dusk.

4. The market is not a wall of archers. It has seen the figure before, and it prices the figure, not the man.

5. Therefore write before you look: the rule, the instrument, the entry, the exits, the size, and the test that would prove the rule false. Date the page. Do not edit it.

6. A rule written after the results is not a rule. It is a caption.

7. Count the trials. Ten filters tried and one kept is one kept out of ten, and the kept one carries the luck of the nine discarded.

8. The pass line must be a number set in advance: this sample, this return, this drawdown, or the idea dies.

9. Pre-registration does not create an edge. It makes the absence of one visible, which is worth more than most edges.

10. He who can kill his own rule on the day the test fails has gained what the rule could never give him: a method.

## Commentary

A backtest is a mirror, not a window. The researcher who tries twenty variations and keeps the one that worked has not tested twenty ideas. He has tested one idea twenty times and reported the luckiest. The reported result is conditioned on the search, and the search is invisible in the equity curve.

The cure is procedural rather than moral. One page, dated before the first run: the universe, the rule, the structure, the exits, the size, the sample window, and the result that counts as failure. After that, no edits. If the rule needs a filter added after the loss appeared, the filter is part of the result, and the test must begin again with the trial count raised.

Two habits make the page honest. Report the search, not only the winner: how many variations, how many symbols, how many windows. And keep a final period untouched until the question is settled, so the arbiter has not already read the argument.

A pre-registered test that fails is not a wasted month. It is the only kind of test that can teach anything, because it was capable of failing. The alternative, a rule that was always going to look good, teaches only that the curve can be made to rise.

# 8. Secretly Cross at Chencang

*Part II: Stratagems for Confronting the Enemy*

1. The plank road into the mountains was burned behind the army, and then rebuilt in plain sight, plank by plank, with the whole world watching the works. The army crossed by Chencang, on a road nobody watched.

2. An earnings date is a plank road. The crowd repairs it for weeks, argues about the number, and on the morning after looks only at the gap.

3. The gap is the noise. The drift is the road, and it begins after the crowd has spent its opinion.

4. Before the print, implied volatility carries the event inside it. After the print, that premium is gone, and the same directional thesis is bought cheaper.

5. Guidance and revisions are the baggage train: they move slowly, in one direction, after the announcement. The drift follows the baggage, not the shout.

6. The road, not the gate: a debit spread two to four weeks out, the near strike bought and the far strike sold, so the event is not paid for twice.

7. Two to four weeks is the length of the road. Shorter, and the drift has not begun; longer, and theta has eaten the fare.

8. The secret is not the direction. It is the date: the second day after the news, not the first.

9. A post-earnings trade without an earnings date is a traveller with another country's map.

10. Before the number, the buyer is one of the archers. After it, he is the courier.

## Commentary

The announcement is the loudest day of the quarter and the least useful to a buyer of direction. The gap reprices the level in a minute. What reprices more slowly is the estimate, the guidance and the positioning around it. That second repricing is the drift, and it takes weeks rather than minutes.

Volatility does the buyer a favour on the way through. Before the print, the option price contains an event premium; after it, the premium collapses. The same two-to-four-week debit spread that was expensive on the day before the number is cheap on the day after. The buyer pays for the road and not again for the gate.

The structure suits a drift because a drift is a bounded move with a date on it. A debit spread caps the payoff, which is a defect only if the thesis is a doubling. Here the thesis is a move of a few per cent over a few weeks, and the short strike is set beyond the move the revisions can plausibly support.

The danger is the gap that has already walked the road. If the print has repriced the year, the drift is in the price before the spread is bought. Drift must be measured in the name, over its own past prints, before it is trusted. A pattern that is famous is also crowded.

# 9. Watch the Fire From Across the River

*Part II: Stratagems for Confronting the Enemy*

1. The house on the far bank is burning. The general does not cross to loot it. He lets the fire finish its argument, and counts what is left when the smoke clears.

2. An event is a fire, and the straddle is the price of standing near it. When the price of standing near is greater than the heat, the far bank is the better post.

3. Across thirty-eight FOMC meetings in recent years, the median move after the decision was smaller than the move the day's straddle had priced. Direction was close to a coin flip.

4. A priced event is a settled argument. The fear is already in the premium; the buyer arrives after the smoke and pays for it.

5. Watching is a position. Flat has a profit and loss of zero and keeps the quiver full.

6. The watcher pays nothing to watch. The buyer of the event pays the straddle, and then pays theta for the days in between.

7. "I have no view" is not the same as "there is no edge". Sometimes the edge is the refusal to pay for one.

8. The exception is a mispriced event, and it must be measured, not assumed. The default is the far bank.

9. The river is the spread and the decay. Every crossing costs; count the cost before calling the fire an opportunity.

10. The general who watches well fights once. The one who crosses at every smoke fights every week, and calls it work.

## Commentary

The FOMC finding is unusually clean. Over thirty-eight meetings, the median move after the decision was smaller than the move the day's straddle had priced, and the direction was roughly a coin flip. A straddle bought into the decision needed the move to beat its own cost, and the typical meeting did not.

The median is not the mean, and the distinction matters. A handful of decisions produce moves far larger than the price, and those few can carry a long-volatility book. The finding says that most meetings are priced, not that every meeting is. It also describes recent years; a priced market is a moving fact, not a law.

What survives is a procedural point. Watching costs nothing and preserves the option to act. Buying an event costs the straddle and then the decay, and it must be justified by a measured mispricing rather than by the size of the coming news. The size of the news is what the straddle already prices.

Hence the discipline: the far bank is the default, and a crossing requires a reason that can be written down before the decision, with the number that would show the price wrong.

# 10. Hide a Dagger Behind a Smile

*Part II: Stratagems for Confronting the Enemy*

1. The host smiles, fills the cup, and speaks of old friendship. The blade is under the sleeve, and the sleeve is long.

2. The far weekly smiles in the same way. A few cents on the screen buy a fortune at the far strike, and the fortune is drawn where the curve almost never goes.

3. The smile is the price. The dagger is the probability.

4. Cheapness is not a gift. It is a quote for a thing that almost never happens, made by people who have counted.

5. Count the smile: the bid-ask paid on entry and again on exit, and the theta that charges rent for every day the far strike stays far.

6. The far strike must be crossed twice, once by the stock and once by the clock. The first is unlikely. The second is certain.

7. Weekly theta is not a slope. It is a cliff, and the last days are its edge.

8. A call whose delta is two in a hundred has, by the market's own rough reckoning, about one chance in fifty of finishing in the money, and needs more than that to pay.

9. Asymmetry is a ratio of payoff to cost, not a feeling about the payoff. A ticket priced at the whole expectancy is not asymmetric.

10. The buyer who wants the tail must buy time with it, or a nearer strike, or nothing. The dagger is in his own hand, and he gives it away at the fill.

## Commentary

The far out-of-the-money weekly is the purest picture of asymmetry on the screen: a few cents against a strike that would pay many times the cost. The picture omits two charges. The first is the spread, paid on the way in and on the way out. The second is theta, which at the far strike is not a slope but a cliff, because the last days of a weekly carry almost all of the remaining extrinsic value.

The volatility smile is the honest name of the cheapness. The surface is not flat: a far strike carries its own implied volatility, and the option is cheap in dollars because the probability is small, not because its volatility is cheap. The buyer is paying for the picture, and the picture is what the seller is selling.

Take a call with a delta of 0.02. The market's rough odds of finishing in the money are about one in fifty; to make money the stock must finish well beyond the strike, not at it. Multiply that by the spread and by the days of decay, and the expectancy is poor before any view about direction is formed.

None of this makes the far strike wrong. It makes it a price. A buyer who wants that payoff must pay for time as well as distance, or move the strike closer, or decline the trade. The dagger is not hidden from him. It is in his hand from the moment of the fill.

# 11. Sacrifice the Plum for the Peach

*Part II: Stratagems for Confronting the Enemy*

1. A worm came to the peach root, and the plum tree beside it took the bite and stiffened in its place. The peach lived.

2. The far strike is the plum. Sell it, and the near strike is bought cheaper; the debit that remains is the whole risk.

3. Buy the 100 call, sell the 110 call, pay 3. The ticket states the count before the market moves: maximum loss 300, maximum gain 700, breakeven 103.

4. Breakeven win rate is the debit divided by the width of the spread. Three in ten at 3; two and a half at 2.50.

5. At 2.50 the trade is 3:1, with 250 at risk and 750 to gain. The arithmetic does not care about the story.

6. The plum sold is the tail above 110, the part of the move the buyer has agreed not to be paid for.

7. A long call at ten-to-one keeps the whole tail and needs one win in eleven. The spread needs three in ten, and a move to 103.

8. Know which tree you are in. If the thesis is a doubling, the spread caps it; if the thesis is a move to 108, the single leg is a donation.

9. The short leg is a loan against conviction, repaid out of the move above the short strike.

10. A spread is not a smaller long. It is a different wager on the same direction: the tail sold, the odds moved.

## Commentary

The plum is the strike sold, and the sacrifice is exact. A ten-wide spread bought for 3 has a maximum loss of 300, a maximum gain of 700 and a breakeven at 103. The breakeven win rate is the debit divided by the width, 3 divided by 10, or 30 per cent. Pay 2.50 instead and the same spread risks 250 to make 750, a ratio of 3:1, and the breakeven win rate falls to 25 per cent.

The single long call bought at ten-to-one has a different count. Risk one to make ten, and one win in eleven is enough to break even, a little over 9 per cent. That looks easier than 30 per cent until the event is named: the ten-to-one ticket needs a near-doubling, and the 3:1 spread needs a move to 103. The spread has traded the tenfold tail for a breakeven price a three-dollar move can reach. Which is better depends on what the thesis actually predicts, not on which payoff is prettier.

This is the arithmetic behind Mai's practice of risking one to make ten, as Schwager records it in Hedge Fund Market Wizards and Lewis in The Big Short. Most of those bets lose; the few that pay carry the book. The buyer who keeps the tenfold tail accepts many losses; the one who sells it accepts that the doubling will be paid to someone else. Mixing the two — a spread sized like a lottery ticket, a lottery ticket held like a spread — is not coherent.

# 12. Take the Goat in Passing

*Part II: Stratagems for Confronting the Enemy*

1. The column passed the village at dusk. A goat stood by the road; a soldier led it away without breaking step.

2. The seller's goat is the volatility premium. It stands by the road most days: implied above realised, the term curve in contango, no event on the calendar.

3. The buyer who pays for calm feeds the seller. Calm is what the seller sells, and calm is usually priced.

4. The goat is taken only when it is untended: after a crush, when implied sits below realised and a dated event still lies ahead.

5. "Quiet" is not "cheap". A stock at a three-year low in realised volatility is a description, not a signal.

6. The mechanical hunt was tested. Twelve-month calls struck 30 per cent out of the money, bought when realised volatility sat at a three-year low, 2020 to 2025, 580 trades.

7. At a realistic price about 58 cents came back per dollar of premium. About 88 per cent expired worthless. The same calls on ordinary stocks did about the same.

8. The test did not fail because quiet is bad. It failed because quiet was only the entry condition; the edge was the judgement of the event, and a screen cannot borrow an eye.

9. A goat is taken in passing, not hunted for a season. The trade is small, on the way, and does not change the march.

10. Most of the time the premium is the seller's. Knowing that is the buyer's first skill; knowing the exception is the second.

## Commentary

The volatility risk premium is the seller's goat. Implied volatility tends to sit above the volatility later realised; the term curve usually sits in contango; and away from a dated event the buyer of protection has little reason to be paid. A buyer who pays for calm is buying insurance in fair weather, at a price set by someone who knows the weather.

The exception has a shape, not a slogan. After a volatility crush, implied can fall below realised. If a dated event still lies ahead, the seller has left the goat untended: the premium no longer covers the risk, and the buyer feeds no one. Only then is the goat on the road at all.

The mechanical version was tested and failed. A pre-registered study bought twelve-month calls struck 30 per cent out of the money whenever a stock's realised volatility was at a three-year low, over 2020 to 2025, 580 trades. At a realistic price, about $0.58 came back per $1 of premium. About 88 per cent expired worthless. The same calls on ordinary stocks did no better; the quiet added nothing.

That clause is the whole finding. The counterweight to Mai's long-dated buying is not that long-dated buying is wrong; it is that quiet was his entry condition, not his edge. In Schwager's Hedge Fund Market Wizards, and in Lewis's The Big Short, the edge is the reading of a particular situation. A screen can find quiet. It cannot borrow the eye that knows which quiet is load-bearing.

# 13. Beat the Grass to Startle the Snake

*Part III: Stratagems for Attack*

1. The beater strikes the grass, not the snake. The rustle tells him where the snake was, and the snake, startled, tells him more than any map.

2. A paper trade is grass beaten at no price. Nothing is at stake, and the ground still answers.

3. Thirty to fifty forward paper trades on one setup, each recorded before money touches it. Forward, not fitted to the past.

4. The first probe with money is minimum size: the smallest position the market will carry. Size is the one variable that cannot be taken back.

5. A probe that costs a real share of the risk budget is not a probe. It is a position wearing a probe's coat.

6. What the beating reveals is not the snake but the ground: whether the strike fills, how wide the spread sits at the open, whether the move arrives on the day.

7. He who startles the snake and writes nothing has learned only fear. The record is the count, and the count is the point.

8. Do not beat every field. One setup, one patch, until its habits are known.

## Commentary

The stratagem is usually read as a warning: do not alert the enemy. For a buyer of options it is better read as a method. The snake is the market's behaviour around a setup — how it fills, when it moves, what it does to a spread — and the only way to see it is to disturb the grass and watch. The disturbance must be cheap, or it is not observation but commitment.

Paper is the cheapest grass. Thirty to fifty forward trades on one setup, each recorded with its entry, its exit and the reason, will tell the trader more than a year of reading. The number is not magic; it is large enough that a single lucky week does not become a method, and small enough that a working setup is not buried under a decade of waiting.

The first money is a probe at minimum size. If it is wrong, the loss is a rounding error and the information is real. If it is right, the trader has confirmed a fill and a behaviour at the smallest possible rent. What must not happen is the probe growing before the count is complete. A probe that becomes a position has stopped asking questions.

# 14. Borrow a Corpse to Return the Soul

*Part III: Stratagems for Attack*

1. The dead man is carried home so that the soul may enter the house it knew. The body is borrowed; only the soul must be living.

2. An old screen is a corpse that still walks. It walks in the direction of the last data it was fed.

3. A short ratio is shares sold short divided by shares available to trade. The numerator is reported; the denominator is filed, and filings age.

4. A ratio of 117 percent is not a signal. It is a division whose denominator was measured years ago, and the dead hand of that measurement still points.

5. Recompute with fresh share counts and the same names fall out of the top three. The formula did not change. The body did.

6. Ask of every input: when was this last measured, by whom, and would a fresh count move the ranking?

7. A backtest fed on restated data is a soul returning to the wrong body, and it will walk convincingly.

8. Old data is the cheapest corpse in the market and the most expensive to trust.

## Commentary

The short-interest screen is a good tool with a bad habit. A short ratio compares the shares sold short with the float, and the two numbers are not measured at the same time. The short interest is published on its own schedule; the share count arrives in filings that may be a year old, or older, and a company that has issued shares since then will appear far more crowded than it is. One screen built this way put names with a 117 percent short ratio at the top. Recalculated with fresh share counts, those names left the top three.

Nothing in the arithmetic was wrong. The division was correct; the denominator was dead. This is the ordinary way a corpse is borrowed: the formula is reused because it worked, and the data underneath it is reused because it is there. The two are not the same age, and the screen cannot tell the difference.

The trader's remedy is a date on every input. Before a screen is believed, each column is asked when it was last true, and what would happen to the ranking if it were refreshed. The soul is the question; the corpse is only the form it returns in.

# 15. Lure the Tiger Down the Mountain

*Part III: Stratagems for Attack*

1. The tiger cannot be fought on its mountain. It is drawn down by bait, to ground chosen before the bait is set.

2. An illiquid strike is the tiger's mountain. The buyer who climbs to it pays the mountain's price, and the mountain keeps the difference.

3. Open interest above five hundred at the strike, volume above one hundred, bid-ask under about five percent of mid. Below these lines, the ground belongs to the tiger.

4. A wide spread is a tax charged before the thesis begins. It is charged whether the thesis is right or wrong.

5. The entry is half the battle; the exit is the other half. A strike that cannot be sold has not been bought.

6. Limit orders at mid. Price is the only term of the trade the buyer can still choose.

7. The chased fill is a loss already taken, paid in advance for haste.

8. Better a small position on open ground than a large one in a cave.

## Commentary

Liquidity is the terrain of this trade, and it is measured before the thesis is argued. Three numbers do most of the work: open interest at the strike, volume on the day, and the width of the bid-ask relative to mid. The thresholds used here are five hundred contracts of open interest, one hundred of volume, and a spread under about five percent of mid. They are not sacred, but they are the difference between a price and an offer.

The spread is charged twice, on the way in and on the way out. A buyer who pays five percent each way has paid ten percent before the underlying has moved at all, and that cost is certain while the thesis is not. This is why the round trip is counted, not the entry, and why the limit order at mid is the default rather than the exception.

The tiger is not the market maker. The tiger is the structure of an empty strike: a quote that widens when the buyer is impatient, a short leg that cannot be closed, a settlement that arrives before the exit. A buyer who cannot leave has not chosen a position; he has been given one.

# 16. To Catch, First Let Go

*Part III: Stratagems for Attack*

1. The net is opened on one side, not from mercy, but so the quarry runs into the side that is closed.

2. A plan made at entry has targets in it. A target named after the price has moved is a wish with a number attached.

3. Release part at the first target: the trade can no longer cost the whole debit, and what has come back is the right to choose again.

4. Release the rest on a date, not on a feeling. The remainder is the only part that can pay for the losers.

5. Freedom is inside the plan. Beyond the plan it is not freedom but improvisation with money.

6. Sell everything at the first target and the buyer has bought a wage, not an asymmetry.

7. Sell nothing and he holds a lottery ticket with a thesis attached to it.

8. Write both lines before entry: the price at which part is released, and the date at which all of it is.

## Commentary

The stratagem is about control, and control in a long option is mostly a matter of having decided in advance what will be let go. A debit is paid once; after that, the position is a series of choices about when to take something back. The buyer who makes those choices at the moment of price is not choosing, he is reacting.

A pre-planned scale-out does one specific thing. It removes the possibility that a trade which was right for a while becomes a total loss, because part of the debit has already returned. What remains is a smaller position with a lower cost basis and a defined date. The time stop is the other half: it releases the remainder whether or not the target was reached, so that the trade ends on the calendar rather than on the trader's mood.

The plan must be written before entry because after entry the mind will negotiate. A target invented after the fact always fits the current price. A date invented after the fact always allows one more week. The buyer who lets go on schedule keeps the only freedom that matters, which is to take the next trade with a clear count.

# 17. Toss a Brick to Attract Jade

*Part III: Stratagems for Attack*

1. The brick is thrown to bring back jade. It is worth little, and that is precisely why it can be thrown.

2. A candidate is a brick. Most are discarded, and the discarding is the work.

3. Thousands come in: every name with a chain, every date on the calendar, every story with a price.

4. The gates stand in order: liquidity, a dated catalyst, volatility, structure, risk, edge. Each gate removes, and none is skipped.

5. Liquidity first, because nothing else can be priced without it. Edge last, because it is the one that fails quietly.

6. Five to ten leave the funnel, at most. Some weeks none leave, and the funnel is still working.

7. A funnel that passes everything has no gates. A funnel that passes nothing has no candidates.

8. The jade is not the number of ideas. It is the one that survives every gate and still looks cheap.

## Commentary

The funnel is the plainest machine in this book, and the one most often abandoned. It begins with everything the calendar and the chain can offer, and it ends with a short list. Between the two are six filters applied in a fixed order, so that the same candidate meets the same questions every week. A gate skipped once is skipped every week thereafter.

The order matters. Liquidity is first because a candidate that cannot be traded cannot be evaluated; a beautiful thesis in a dead strike is a story, not a trade. The dated catalyst comes next, because a buyer of options is paying for time and needs to know what the time is for. Volatility, structure and risk narrow the expression. Edge is last, and it is last on purpose: it is the filter most easily fooled by a good narrative, and the one applied to a candidate that has already survived the cheaper tests.

Five to ten names at the end is a full week's work, and often the honest number is zero. The funnel is not failing when it produces nothing. It is failing when it produces fifteen, because that means a gate was left open. The list is not a watchlist. It is the residue of a test, and it is allowed to be empty.

# 18. To Catch Bandits, Catch Their King

*Part III: Stratagems for Attack*

1. A band of thieves is not broken by chasing every man. Take the chief, and the band loses its head and its plan.

2. Fifteen positions are fifteen bandits. Each is small, each is loud, and none of them is the king.

3. One tested setup is the king. One or two independent theses are his court.

4. Independence is not a label. Two call spreads in the same sector are one thesis wearing two tickers.

5. A lottery ticket is a position that cannot be wrong in any way the holder would notice.

6. Put the risk where the evidence is, not where the count is. A large book of small bets is still one bet on being busy.

7. Test the king: the entry, the exit, the failure. The bandits need no test; they will lose on their own.

8. The account that watches fifteen things watches nothing.

## Commentary

Concentration here is not a matter of one large position. It is a matter of attention and of independent evidence. A buyer can hold several small positions and still be doing one thing, if all of them rise and fall on the same rate decision, the same sector, the same crowded short. The count of positions says nothing; the count of independent theses says everything. Attention is the scarce capital, and it does not diversify.

The king is the setup that has been tested and can be described in the five sentences: edge, catalyst, maximum loss, time stop, breakeven win rate. A court of one or two independent theses is enough to work with. Beyond that, the trader is not diversifying, he is collecting lottery tickets, and each ticket demands the same attention as the king while offering no more evidence.

The test is applied to the king, not to every bandit. A setup is tested once, in paper and then at minimum size, and the result is recorded. The fifteen small positions that were never tested are not a portfolio. They are a way of avoiding the question of whether any of them works. Ask the question once, of the king, and the band is easier to read.

# 19. Remove the Firewood From Under the Pot

*Part IV: Stratagems for Confused Situations*

1. To still a boiling pot, no one ladles the water. A man draws the firewood from beneath it, and the boil dies of itself.

2. A loss is the boiling; the size that feeds it is the firewood. To argue with the price after entry is to ladle, and the pot boils faster for it.

3. Before the first order, write the worst case in one sentence: *my worst case is three hundred dollars, because the debit is three.*

4. For a long option the debit is the whole risk. For a debit spread it is the width less the credit. For stock it is the distance to the stop, times the shares.

5. Divide the risk budget by the worst case of one unit, and round down. What remains is not idle money; it is the firewood left outside the wall.

6. If one contract is over the budget, take a narrower spread, a smaller underlying, or no trade. Never round up to one.

7. Risk one to make ten is a constraint before it is a hope: the one must be small enough to lose ten times in a row and still sit down to count the eleventh.

8. In his interview in Jack Schwager's *Hedge Fund Market Wizards*, Jamie Mai described a book of such bets, most of them expected to fail, the few that paid carrying all the rest. The arithmetic begins with the one, not the ten.

9. A three-dollar spread wins seven and loses three. That is the pot. How many pots the account can feed at once is the firewood.

10. The pot does not know why it cools. The market owes no man a reason, only the bill.

## Commentary

The stratagem strikes at the cause of the boil, not at the boil. In a trade the visible thing is the loss on the screen; the cause is the size that made the loss matter. A trader can spend an afternoon reasoning with a losing spread — the thesis is intact, the date is close, the chart sits on support — and every argument is a ladle. The firewood is untouched underneath: too many contracts for the worst case.

The arithmetic is easy, which is why it is so often skipped. A 100 call bought and a 110 call sold for a $3 debit: the width is $10, the debit $3, so the maximum loss is $300 and the maximum gain $700. If the risk budget is $1,000, three spreads cost $900 and the fourth costs $1,200. It is $200 over, so the count is three. Rounding up to four does not make the risk $1,000. It makes the plan a fiction.

Mai's version of risk one to make ten is often quoted as though the ten were the point. In Schwager's interview the one comes first, and it is small. The bets are expected to fail, and a book has to survive a run of failures to collect the one that does not. Ten bets at a tenth each, all lost, is the whole book. That is not convexity; it is ten chances to be finished before the thesis arrives.

# 20. Fish in Troubled Waters

*Part IV: Stratagems for Confused Situations*

1. The fisherman who cannot see the bottom stirs the mud. The fish rise gasping, and the net does the rest.

2. In a high-volatility market the water is already muddy. Protection and lottery tickets are bought in the same hour, and both are marked up.

3. Premium is dearest when buying feels most urgent. The crowd is not paying for information; it is paying for its own alarm.

4. To reach for a long single leg in that water is to buy the alarm at its peak. The position carries its largest vega at the moment it is least needed.

5. Fish with the spread. The strike that is sold returns part of the inflated premium, and the vega of the position is a difference between two vegas, not one.

6. A debit spread in muddy water is a net with a lid. Less is paid, less has to be true, and the same move still pays.

7. The far bank is the seller's ground: naked puts, ratios, income promised in a drawdown. Even there, only defined risk. The buyer's book has no business crossing.

8. Muddy water is not the buyer's ally. It is his price, and he beats it only by paying less for it than the crowd.

## Commentary

The stratagem has the fisherman make the mud himself. The market needs no help. A volatility shock muddies the water for everyone, and the fear that lifts implied volatility lifts the price of every structure built on it. Demand for protection and demand for lottery tickets arrive together. The buyer who acts on urgency pays the top of both.

The buyer's error here is structural rather than directional. A long single leg carries the full vega of the chain's fear. A debit spread carries the difference between two vegas, and the strike that is sold hands back part of the inflated premium. The view can be identical; the price of admission is not. In muddy water the spread is the cheaper way to hold the same thesis, which is the whole of the argument and none of the forecast.

The far bank is the seller's book. A buyer who crosses over becomes a seller without a seller's collateral or temperament, and the mud is deepest exactly where the income looks easiest. The buyer's discipline is narrower: keep the risk defined, keep the position small enough that the mud cannot swallow it, and let the water clear before judging the trade.

# 21. The Golden Cicada Sheds Its Shell

*Part IV: Stratagems for Confused Situations*

1. The cicada does not die when it leaves the shell. The husk stays on the branch, whole and empty, and the bird strikes it.

2. A position carried into expiry is that husk. The shape is still on the screen; the life has gone out of it.

3. Leave the spread before the last days, not on them. The final week is where an illiquid short leg is pinned against a strike with nowhere to go.

4. An illiquid short leg held into the pin is no longer a position. It is a place in a queue at the settlement window.

5. Know what settles, and how. Broad index options settle in cash, some at the morning print and some at the close. An ETF option is a claim on shares, and shares can be assigned.

6. The difference is not academic. One expires into cash; the other can expire into a delivery, a margin call, or a short position nobody planned.

7. The trader who writes the exit at entry keeps the choice of the shell. The one who waits for expiry hands that choice to the clearing house.

8. An empty husk is a good disguise for a trader who has already gone.

## Commentary

Expiry is a decision whether or not it is made. A spread left open into the last week is not being held; it is being handed over, and the terms are set by whoever is on the other side of the illiquid leg. The short strike that looked harmless at entry becomes a pin, and the market for closing it becomes a queue.

The settlement distinction is where buyers are most often surprised. Cash-settled index options end as a calculation against an index print; some use the morning print, some the close. ETF options end as claims on shares. A short call there can be assigned before expiry, leaving a short stock position and a margin requirement that were never in the plan. The buyer who does not know which of the two he holds has not finished counting the trade.

None of this requires a forecast. It requires a date, written at entry, on which the spread is closed or rolled, and a habit of checking the contract's settlement terms before the position is opened. The golden cicada is not cleverer than the bird. It is simply gone before the strike.

# 22. Shut the Door to Catch the Thief

*Part IV: Stratagems for Confused Situations*

1. When the thief is in the house, shut the door. Do not chase him into the fields, where the ground knows him better than it knows you.

2. The plan is the door, and it shuts at entry. After that the thief is inside terms already written.

3. Do not move the stop because the price came close. Do not widen the spread because the debit became uncomfortable. Do not extend the time stop because the thesis is "still intact".

4. "Give it room" is the thief's phrase, spoken in the trader's voice.

5. Tightening is permitted. A smaller loss is still a loss taken by plan. Loosening is the theft.

6. A plan that may be revised under loss is not a plan. It is a preference with a schedule.

7. The door has one hinge: the maximum loss was fixed before entry, and nothing after entry is new information about it. News is information about the thesis, not about the risk.

8. A thief caught in a shut room is a cost. A thief let into the fields is a career.

## Commentary

The stratagem is about containment. A thief inside a closed room can be dealt with at leisure; a thief who reaches open ground must be chased on his own terms. The trader's open ground is the space between the stop he wrote and the stop he is now inventing. Every step into it is a step away from the counted trade and toward a position whose worst case is unknown again.

The three forbidden moves are the same move. Moving the stop, widening the spread, extending the time stop — each takes a loss that was measured at entry and makes it unmeasured at the worst possible moment, when the position is already losing. They rarely arrive as a decision. They arrive as a sentence: the thesis needs one more week, the strike was too close, the earnings date slipped. The sentence is always reasonable. That is what makes it the thief.

Tightening is the one revision allowed, because it cannot enlarge the worst case. A stop brought closer, a spread closed early, a time stop honoured — these are still acts of the plan, made in the plan's direction. But the door has to be shut once, at entry, or the room has no walls at all.

# 23. Befriend the Distant, Attack the Near

*Part IV: Stratagems for Confused Situations*

1. The far state is courted with silk and a treaty; the near field is taken acre by acre. The far friend is bought so that the near war may be finished.

2. Five bullish call spreads in five technology names look like five distant states. They answer to one throne.

3. Correlation is the road between them. When one rate, one index, one customer or one fear moves all five, the five are one bet.

4. Count them as one bet, and give that one bet one risk budget, not five.

5. Portfolio heat is what all open trades lose together, not what each loses alone.

6. Keep that heat at two or three per cent of the account. Past it, the fifth spread is not diversification; it is the same bet bought again at a worse price.

7. The distant ally is the trade that shares no catalyst with the rest: another driver, another date, another mechanism.

8. A general who mistakes one enemy for five wins five times on paper and loses once in fact.

## Commentary

The stratagem is usually read as a map of alliances. For the buyer it is a map of exposure. Five call spreads in five names that rise and fall on the same rate, the same index, the same customer or the same liquidity feel like five positions and are one. The screen counts them as five; the account counts them as one when the factor moves.

The arithmetic of heat is where the illusion is paid for. If each spread risks one per cent, five correlated spreads risk five per cent together, and the ceiling for the whole book is two or three per cent. The fifth does not add a new chance; it adds size to a chance already held. In a stress move correlations rise rather than fall, so the diversification that looked real at entry is least real when it is needed.

The distant ally is not a different ticker. It is a different reason: a different catalyst, a different date, a different mechanism, a different sensitivity to the same rate. A book of five names and one reason is a single trade with five commissions. A book of two names and two reasons is two trades.

# 24. Borrow the Road to Conquer Guo

*Part IV: Stratagems for Confused Situations*

1. Jin borrowed the road through Yu to reach Guo. The road was granted, Guo fell, and on the march home Yu fell with it.

2. Two wars were fought on one road, and the host learned what a borrowed road costs: no fixed term, and no safe return.

3. Single-stock options and index options are two wars. They share a screen and a vocabulary, not a method.

4. The single name asks: which company catalyst, which dated event, whose guidance, what skew, what borrow.

5. The index asks: which macro date, what term structure, what variance risk premium, where gamma is parked, how the contract settles.

6. A calendar built for one is a road through the other's country. The army arrives, and the ground does not answer.

7. An edge tested on one name does not become an index rule by being applied to more names.

8. March each army on its own road. Borrow nothing but the discipline.

## Commentary

The stratagem ends with the host annexed, and that is the part the buyer should keep. The borrowed road is convenient while the campaign is going well; it is the return march that reveals who owns the ground. A method borrowed from one market and applied to another has the same shape: it works until the terrain that made it work is missing.

The two pipelines are different in their inputs. A single name is moved by a company's dated events: earnings, guidance, a regulator, a court, a deal, a borrow rate that tightens. An index is moved by the macro calendar, by the shape of the term structure, by the price of variance itself, and by where dealers are hedged. One pipeline screens for a catalyst and a skew; the other screens for a date and a premium. A funnel built for the first will pass candidates the second cannot price.

The practical rule is dull. Two calendars, two sets of filters, two pre-registered tests, two journals. A result earned on single names is evidence about single names; carrying it across the road is how a working method becomes a loss in a market it never met.

# 25. Replace the Beams With Rotten Timbers

*Part V: Stratagems for Gaining Ground*

1. A hall is raised beam by beam. Swap the beams for rotten timber, paint them true, and the roof still sits — until the first snow.

2. A backtest is such a hall. Its beams are the trades it counts; the load it must bear is the trade actually placed.

3. The substitution is quiet. A hit rate is put where a win rate belongs: that a stock doubled within a quarter is a fact about the stock, not the record of a stopped trade.

4. A hit rate counts arrivals. A trade counts exits — the premium paid, the date of the stop, the price at which the spread can be sold.

5. So each expression must be tested on its own timber: the stock, the long call, the debit spread, the spread at the price the buyer can actually pay.

6. The second rotten beam is a rule that was never a rule. "Buy cheap convexity when the stock is quiet" sounds like a method. It is one man's judgement, compressed into a sentence by someone else.

7. Jamie Mai, as he describes his practice in Schwager's *Hedge Fund Market Wizards* and as Lewis tells it in *The Big Short*, was a buyer of cheap convexity. The quiet was his entry condition. The event was his edge.

8. Remove the event and the screen is a roof with nothing beneath it. A screen cannot borrow an eye.

9. When the beam is judgement, no filter cut from it will carry the same load.

## Commentary

The first substitution is arithmetic. A study counted how often a stock doubled within sixty-three sessions and found it happened 12.6% of the time among crowded-short picks, against 1.6% for random picks. That is a hit rate at the stock level. It says nothing about the win rate of a stopped option trade on the same names: the premium, the time stop and the exit price all sit between the two numbers. Present the first as the second and the house stands on rotten timber.

The second substitution is harder to see because it wears the clothes of a rule. A pre-registered test took Mai's entry condition — a stock whose realized volatility sat at a three-year low — and bought twelve-month calls struck 30% out of the money, 2020 to 2025, 580 trades. At a realistic price the test returned about $0.58 per $1 of premium; about 88% expired worthless; the calls did no better than the same calls on ordinary stocks.

What the test removed was the beam that carried the roof. Mai's quiet stock was a place to look, not a reason to buy. The reason was an event he had judged — a court, a regulator, a solvency question — and a screen has no eye for that. Each expression needs its own test. When the beam is judgement, the test can only tell you that the timber you substituted was rotten.

# 26. Point at the Mulberry, Curse the Locust

*Part V: Stratagems for Gaining Ground*

1. A man points at the mulberry and curses it. The locust on the next branch hears, and mends its ways. The words are aimed at one tree; the correction is meant for another.

2. A trade card speaks in the same figure. It names the evidence and names the absence of evidence; the reader is meant to hear both.

3. "Research, not yet proven, paper only" is not a warning label stuck to the card. It is the card.

4. A label that claims less than the evidence is honest. A label that claims more is a curse uttered at no tree in particular.

5. Say what was tested: which structure, which years, which costs, which exits, which sample.

6. Say what was not: the sample too small, the regime untested, the fill assumed at mid.

7. The seam is where the roof opens. A trader who hides the seam invites the reader to lean on it.

8. A false label is cheap to write and expensive to hold. The trade remembers what the card forgot.

9. Praise the mulberry beyond its fruit, and the locust learns to trust false praise.

10. The tree pointed at must be a real tree. Otherwise the listener learns only to watch the finger.

## Commentary

The stratagem is usually read as discipline by indirection: correct one man by correcting another in his hearing. The trade card works the same way. It is written for the next reader of the position — a partner, a future self, a reviewer — and its honesty is measured by what it declines to claim.

A card that says "pilot, one winner, mean negative" tells the reader how much weight the number can bear. A card that says "edge confirmed" on the same evidence tells him nothing except that the writer wanted it confirmed. The difference is not modesty. It is load-bearing: the label determines whether the result is repeated, reduced or killed.

The trader's own labels matter most when no one else reads them. A journal entry that says "research, paper only" keeps the position in the category where it belongs; an entry that promotes it to "system" before the sample supports it moves real risk onto a beam that has not been tested. An honest label is not a weakness of the card. It is the card.

# 27. Feign Madness but Keep Your Balance

*Part V: Stratagems for Gaining Ground*

1. The general wears the fool's mask in camp and keeps his accounts by night. The folly is feigned; the balance is real.

2. A day with no qualifying trade looks like a wasted day. It is a position, and often the right one.

3. The funnel admits thousands and returns five. On many days it returns none. The none is the funnel working.

4. To invent a candidate so that the day has a trade is to mistake activity for edge.

5. Feign indifference before the market. Keep the count in private: the five sentences, the calendar, the size.

6. An empty board is not an empty mind. It is a board that has refused every candidate that could not pay.

7. The hand that must act to feel useful will pay the market for the feeling.

8. Folly that believes itself is no longer feigned. A trader who acts the fool until he forgets the count has simply become one.

9. Wait. The market opens again tomorrow, and the count does not expire with the session.

10. To come home empty-handed and keep the measure is skill, not luck.

## Commentary

The stratagem is about a mask worn with a purpose: the appearance of disorder covering a real order. For the buyer the mask is idleness. A session in which nothing passes the funnel looks, from outside, like a session in which nothing was done. From inside it is the last stage of the funnel, where the candidates that survived liquidity and calendar and volatility are rejected for want of an edge.

The funnel from the seventeenth stratagem runs thousands of names down to five or ten. On a quiet day it can run them down to zero. The trader who cannot tolerate zero invents a candidate: a chart that almost qualifies, a catalyst that might arrive, a spread a little too wide. Each of these is a real cost, paid for the comfort of having acted.

The discipline is to record the empty day as a result. The journal should show the candidates reviewed and the reason each was refused; then the empty day is evidence of work rather than absence of it. A board with nothing on it can be the most honest board in the book.

# 28. Remove the Ladder When the Enemy Has Climbed

*Part V: Stratagems for Gaining Ground*

1. Invite a man onto the roof, then take the ladder away. He goes forward or he leaps; retreat has been priced out.

2. The buyer builds his ladder before he climbs: a first tranche, then rungs at prices written in advance.

3. The rungs are confirmation levels, not consolation levels. They are climbed when the thesis confirms, never because the price has fallen.

4. The ladder is measured against the maximum loss before the first step. An add that would breach the budget is a rung nailed outside the house.

5. Once on the roof, the ladder comes away. No add for boredom, no add for a headline, no add because the first tranche is red.

6. Averaging down is not a ladder. It is a rope tied to the wrong beam.

7. The plan may tighten; it may not lengthen. A ladder that grows after entry is a staircase to the cellar.

8. Commitment is not the size of the first step. It is the refusal to cut a new rung afterward.

9. The enemy on the roof is not the market. It is the part of the trader that wants a second chance at a better price.

10. Only when the ladder is cleanly gone does the man see the roof he stands on.

## Commentary

The stratagem removes the option of retreat so that the man on the roof commits to the route he chose. In the buyer's trade the ladder is built in the opposite direction: it is placed before entry, and taken away after. The first tranche is small because it is a step, not a verdict. The adds are written at confirmation levels, where the thesis has shown something, and they are funded from the original maximum-loss budget rather than from a fresh one.

That last clause is the roof. If the budget for the whole position is fixed before the first contract, then every add draws against the same account, and a ladder that exceeds it is not commitment but a second, larger trade smuggled in under the first. The budget does not move because the first tranche is losing; that is precisely when a new rung feels most deserved.

Averaging down is the clearest case. It lowers the average cost and raises the amount at risk, and it does so at the moment the thesis is least confirmed. The plan after entry is allowed to tighten — a smaller size, an earlier time stop — and not to lengthen. After the climb there is no new ladder.

# 29. Deck the Tree With False Blossoms

*Part V: Stratagems for Gaining Ground*

1. Silk blossoms are tied to a bare tree. From the road it is spring; up close it is string and dye.

2. A sample can be decked the same way. One spread that returned several times its debit makes a season.

3. Look at the median, not the blossom. The middle trade is what the method usually does.

4. Look at coverage: how many candidates the rule found, how many it could fill, how many it counted.

5. Look at the whole distribution, including the worthless expiries and the trades never entered.

6. In the options pilot, one spread at +148% stood in a sample whose median was −94%. The mean was a blossom; the median was the tree.

7. Jamie Mai's practice, as he describes it in Schwager's *Hedge Fund Market Wizards* and as Lewis tells it in *The Big Short*, was built to look like this: risk one to make ten, expect most bets to lose, let the few carry the book.

8. That is a description of a distribution, not a promise about the next trade.

9. The question is never "did most win". It is whether the few paid for all the rest, after costs, in a sample large enough to trust.

10. A tree with one blossom is not in bloom. It is a tree with one blossom.

## Commentary

The pilot is the plainest warning here. One spread returned 148%, and the sample's median was a loss of 94%. Anyone shown the winner alone would call the method promising; anyone shown the median would call it broken. Both numbers are true, and the gap between them is the whole question of sample size.

The mean of such a sample can be positive while the typical trade is a near-total loss, because a single large winner carries the average. That is not a defect of the arithmetic. It is the shape of the buyer's trade, and it is the shape Mai described: many small losses, a few large gains, the book carried by the few. In Schwager's interview he presents this as a way of thinking about payoff, not as a forecast; Lewis's account tells the same story of cheap positions held against unlikely outcomes.

So the test of the method is not "did most win". It is whether the winners paid for all the losers, after costs, in a sample large enough that one blossom cannot be mistaken for spring. A tree with one blossom is not in bloom. It is a tree with one blossom.

# 30. Make the Host and the Guest Exchange Roles

*Part V: Stratagems for Gaining Ground*

1. The guest comes in by the side door, stays for supper, and by morning is answering the door as host.

2. In the trade, the guest is the process: the calendar, the five sentences, the size, the time stop, the journal. It arrives as a visitor to one position and must end as the master of the house.

3. The journal records facts, not verdicts. "The stop was moved" is a fact. "The trader is a fool" is a verdict, and a verdict teaches nothing.

4. Count the week: the number of trades, the expectancy after costs, the deepest drawdown, the rules broken.

5. Expectancy is arithmetic, not a feeling: p × average win − (1 − p) × average loss − costs.

6. A broken rule is a datum. Record it beside the profit and loss, not in place of it.

7. The process is itself the first asymmetric bet: downside capped at the risk budget, commitment staged, time stop written before entry.

8. When the process keeps the house, the trade is a guest. When the trade keeps the house, the process has been shown the door.

9. The host need not be right about the guest. He need only know when the guest is to leave.

## Commentary

The stratagem reverses guest and host: the visitor who was tolerated becomes the authority in the house. In a trading account the same reversal is either the whole of the discipline or the whole of the failure. The process begins as something the trader consults — a checklist, a calendar, a size rule — and it either ends by governing the trades or by being ignored whenever a trade is urgent.

The weekly count is where the reversal is made or lost. Four numbers are enough: how many trades, what expectancy after costs, the deepest drawdown, how many rules were broken. Expectancy is p × average win, minus (1 − p) × average loss, minus costs, and p is estimated from the trader's own sample, which is small and uncertain. The formula does not remove the uncertainty; it makes the estimate visible, so that a good week and a good method are not confused.

The broken-rule count is the one most often left out. A week with a profit and three violations is not a good week for the process, whatever the statement says. Written beside the profit and loss, the violations show whether the result came from the method or from the departure. That is the sense in which the pipeline is the first asymmetric bet: its downside is capped by the maximum loss, its commitment is staged, and its time stop is set before there is anything to be impatient about.

# 31. The Beauty Trap

*Part VI: Stratagems for Desperate Situations*

1. A beauty is not found; she is prepared — the dress, the lamp, the angle of the face. So is a backtest.

2. The lord who forgets the pass when the beauty enters has already lost it. The trader who forgets the sample when the curve rises to the right will pay for the lesson.

3. Test her in the room where she was found, then in a room she has never seen. Fix the rule on one period, validate it on another, and keep a third untouched to the last day.

4. The untouched period is the only honest witness. Its door opens once. The trader who peeks and adjusts has moved the door and called it a wall.

5. A hundred cases may be evidence, if the data supply them. Ten cases and a smooth line are a portrait, not a test.

6. Every cut of the data leaves a fingerprint: the window that begins after the low, the strike chosen because it worked, the filter added on the third attempt.

7. A rule and its data, chosen together, cannot acquit each other. The rule is written before the period is opened, as the bet is sized before the outcome is known.

8. Prefer the curve with a scar in it. A method that never lost in the sample was not tested; it was arranged.

9. The buyer needs this discipline more than the seller, because his winners are rare and large. One beautiful run of long calls is exactly what a fitted sample produces.

10. So he asks not whether she is beautiful, but in how many rooms she has been seen, by whom, and with the door left closed.

## Commentary

Walk-forward is not a refinement of backtesting. It is the difference between a test and a memory. The rule is fixed on the first stretch of history, then walked forward one period at a time and judged only on what it meets. A final holdout is held back from every hand. Where the data allow, a hundred historical cases give a distribution something to stand on; fewer, and the beauty is one pose repeated.

The trap is not that the backtest lies. It is that it answers a narrower question than the one asked. In-sample, the data have already been seen by the person choosing the filters, so the fit is a record of that seeing. Out-of-sample, the rule meets prices no one chose. The gap between the two is the price of the fitting, and it is usually the whole of the edge.

For an options buyer the temptation is sharper because the payoff is asymmetric. A handful of enormous winners can make a decade of long calls look like a method, and the eye goes to them. The question to put to the sample is the one put to the beauty: in how many rooms, with how many doors left shut. A rule that exists only in the room where it was found is a portrait. It can be lovely. It cannot be traded.

# 32. The Empty Fort

*Part VI: Stratagems for Desperate Situations*

1. The gates of the city stood open, the streets were swept, and a man sat on the wall with his lute. What turned the enemy was not the garrison. It was the absence of one.

2. An empty fort cannot be dressed. A pretended garrison is found out; an open gate is not.

3. In a drawdown the account has nothing to show. Show nothing: fewer trades, smaller, slower.

4. The position not taken cannot lose. Its maximum loss is known to the cent, and it needs no stop.

5. Cash is not a failure of nerve. It is the wall the next trade stands behind.

6. When the options fort is empty — no strike, no bid, no open interest — it is not garrisoned with a market order.

7. The same thesis can hold another wall: a company priced below its net cash is a fort whose gates open from the inside.

8. A buyer of cheap convexity wants the cheapest expression of the outcome, and the cheapest is not always an option.

9. The empty fort is still a position, and it needs a time stop: held until the enemy passes, not forever.

10. Doing nothing, done correctly, is the only thing being done.

## Commentary

The empty fort is not a trick that works because the enemy is stupid. It works because the fort is genuinely empty, and the open gate is the truth. A drawdown is the same. When the account has no edge that meets the standard, the honest posture is no position. A small position taken to look busy is the pretended garrison, and it is found out at the next open.

No position is the cleanest arithmetic in the book. Maximum loss is zero, no stop is needed, and time decay has nothing to charge. Every cost the buyer carries begins with holding. To stand aside is not to forgo the game. It is to keep the capital that the next dated catalyst will require.

Sometimes the fort is not empty but untenable. There is no chain, no bid, no strike worth the spread, and the options road has no wall to hold. Then the same judgement can be expressed in another instrument. A company trading below its net cash is a fort whose gate opens from within, because the buyer's price already contains the cash. Jamie Mai, as described in Jack Schwager's Hedge Fund Market Wizards, is a buyer of cheap convexity who looks for the cheapest instrument that captures the outcome; when options do not serve, the stock itself can. Lewis's The Big Short tells the same practice as patience rather than a signal.

# 33. Let the Enemy's Spy Sow Discord

*Part VI: Stratagems for Desperate Situations*

1. A letter left on the desk for the spy to steal has done its work before it is read; the hand that kills is the enemy's own.

2. The option chain is the enemy's spy in your camp, reporting daily what others fear: implied move, skew, term structure, the price of borrow.

3. An intercepted report is useful twice: for what it says, and for why someone wished it said.

4. The implied move is a price, not a prophecy. It is what the crowd paid to insure itself against its own fear.

5. Skew is the spy's accent. It tells you which tail he is afraid of.

6. Term structure is the spy's calendar: next week's fear and next year's fear are priced apart.

7. Stale data is a double agent. It answers with yesterday's map and charges today's price.

8. A good map is worth paying for, and the cost of the map belongs inside the trade.

9. The deepest secret is the shape: a smooth curve laid over an event that can only go two ways.

10. Read where the spy's map is wrong, and let the enemy's intelligence point your road.

## Commentary

The stratagem is not bribery. It is making the other side's own judgement do your work. The chain reports a great deal: open interest, volume, implied volatility, the slope of skew, the spread between near and far months, the difficulty of borrowing. None of it is advice. It is a record of what other people paid to be afraid of, and its use is to show where that fear is priced too high or too low.

The implied move is the plainest report. The at-the-money straddle divided by the stock price is what the market has set for one event; set against the last eight to twelve realised moves, it gives a ratio. The ratio is arithmetic. The direction is not. A stale map — old filings, old share counts, an old borrow fee — is worse than no map, because it is charged at today's price.

The shape is the deeper reading. When an outcome is binary — a court ruling, a regulator's decision, a solvency question — the stock has two futures, but option prices are built on one smooth curve. The curve smears two futures into a continuum, and the ends are mispriced. That is the gap Jamie Mai describes in his interview in Jack Schwager's Hedge Fund Market Wizards: a buyer of cheap convexity looking at a two-humped world priced as one hump. The spy's map is wrong at the edges, and that is where it is read.

# 34. Inflict Injury on Oneself to Win Trust

*Part VI: Stratagems for Desperate Situations*

1. A general is beaten before his own camp and goes over to the enemy with the wounds still open. He is believed because the wounds are real.

2. The stop is the buyer's wound, taken by his own hand before the enemy can take it for him.

3. A small loss paid at the stated price buys what the market does not sell: the right to remain in the war.

4. The trader who will not bleed a little bleeds all of it, and is believed by no one, least of all himself.

5. Thirty to fifty trades are the only wound that teaches. Below that, the sample is an opinion.

6. Count the hit rate against the breakeven win rate, not against the last good trade.

7. If after the count the setup is not above that line by a comfortable margin, it is killed. A rule is not a mood.

8. A setup you cannot kill is the one that kills you. It will ask for one more trade, and then one more.

9. The kill rule is written while the setup is still beautiful. Written afterwards, it is a eulogy.

10. The buyer's edge is rare and large. A low hit rate is not a shame; a setup that never clears its line is a habit.

## Commentary

The first injury is the stop. It is taken voluntarily, at a price written before the entry, and its purpose is not to please the market but to keep the account's word intact. A trader who has paid a stop at the stated price knows something about himself that no winning trade teaches. A trader who has widened it once knows something worse.

The second is the count. After thirty to fifty trades a setup has a hit rate, and a hit rate means nothing until it is set against the breakeven win rate. On a debit spread the breakeven win rate is the debit divided by the width of the strikes, so the comparison is arithmetic and can be made without an opinion. Compare the two numbers, and leave room for the costs. A hit rate that only just clears the line is a hit rate that does not clear it.

The third injury is the kill. A setup traded for a year becomes part of the trader's idea of himself, and killing it feels like admitting the year was wasted. So it is granted one more trade. The rule is written at the beginning, when the setup is still a hypothesis and not an identity. A method that cannot be killed is not a method. It is a habit with a story attached.

# 35. Chain Stratagems

*Part VI: Stratagems for Desperate Situations*

1. The ships were lashed together so the fleet would ride steady in the swell. The steadiness was the gift, and one fire was the price.

2. A trade is a chain: the calendar, the five sentences, the size, the alert, the log, the review. One weak link and the rest are cargo.

3. Sunday: the dated events on one calendar, the volatility ranks, the liquidity, five to ten candidates. No more.

4. Before each trade the five sentences are said: edge, catalyst, maximum loss, time stop, breakeven win rate. A missing sentence is a missing link.

5. Size is a link, not an afterthought: the risk budget divided by the maximum loss per unit, rounded down.

6. The alert is a link. The exit is written while the head is cool, not decided while the screen is red.

7. After the trade, the log. Facts, not verdicts.

8. Each week, the review: count, expectancy after costs, maximum drawdown, rule violations.

9. A chain is not a mood. It is a routine that runs in the weeks you do not feel like running it.

10. The buyer's week is the chain, and the chain is what makes the rare winner findable.

## Commentary

The stratagem is usually told as one clever plan nested inside another. Its real point is the linking. No single ring is impressive, and together they hold a fleet. A trading week is built the same way. An hour on Sunday puts the dated events on one calendar, ranks the volatility, checks the liquidity and leaves five to ten candidates; before each entry the five sentences are said aloud; the size is divided down; the alert is set; the log is written; the week is reviewed. None of these is an edge by itself. Together they turn a rare winner into a repeatable process.

The loosest link is usually not analysis but the alert. A trader is calm at entry and is not calm when the screen turns red. An exit written at entry is a plan; an exit invented later is a reaction. The second loosest is the log. "I moved my stop" is a fact and can be counted. "I was an idiot" is a verdict and can only be repeated.

A chain is as strong as its weakest link, which is the whole meaning of the image. A time stop missing from the five sentences leaves the trade with nothing but hope; a size rounded up once undoes every calculation before it. The buyer's time is his enemy, and the chain is his supply line. Cut the line, and no position at the front can be held.

# 36. If All Else Fails, Retreat

*Part VI: Stratagems for Desperate Situations*

1. Of the thirty-six, the last is to march away: not the proudest, but the one that keeps the army.

2. When "I have to make it back" speaks, the defeat is no longer on the field. It is in the man.

3. Revenge is a position with no stop and no catalyst.

4. Retreat to paper, or to a size too small to hurt, until the trades can be read without shame.

5. Shame is the enemy in the rear. A trade you cannot bear to look at cannot be reviewed.

6. The account that leaves the field returns to it. The account that stays to prove something does not.

7. Retreat is a time stop set on the self, written before the drawdown deepens.

8. No position is important enough to be held after the judgement has gone.

9. The last stratagem is the first: know the way out before you take the field.

10. What comes home is three things: the capital, the log, and the habit of returning.

## Commentary

Retreat is placed last because it is what remains when the other thirty-five have failed. It is placed first in importance for the same reason. In a drawdown the danger is not the percentage lost. It is the hurry that arrives with it, the sense that the next trade must repair the last one. That hurry makes positions larger, horizons shorter and stops looser, all at once. The move that keeps the judgement is often to close, or to trade so small that the next loss is a loss and not a verdict.

There are two retreats. One is to paper: real prices, real dates, real size, no money. It separates judgement from emotion and lets the sample grow while the account stops bleeding. The other is to minimum size, small enough that a loss is an expense. Neither is a departure from the market. Both are a departure from the self that took over.

"Until the trades can be read without shame" is the technical part of the stratagem. A review needs facts: why the entry was taken, what the maximum loss was, whether the time stop was honoured, whether the stop was moved. Shame rewrites all of it as a verdict, and verdicts cannot be counted. The account leaves the field so that judgement can come back. Judgement comes back, and the field is still there.
