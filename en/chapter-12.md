# 12. Take the Goat in Passing

*Part II: Stratagems for Confronting the Enemy*

1. The column passed the village at dusk. A goat stood by the road; a soldier led it away without breaking step.

2. The seller's goat is the volatility premium. It stands by the road most days: implied above realised, the term curve in contango, no event on the calendar.

3. The buyer who pays for calm feeds the seller. Calm is what the seller sells, and calm is usually priced.

4. The goat is taken only when it is untended: after a crush, when implied sits below realised and a dated event still lies ahead.

5. "Quiet" is not "cheap". A stock at a three-year low in realised volatility is a description, not a signal.

6. The mechanical hunt was tested. Twelve-month calls struck 30 per cent out of the money, bought when realised volatility sat at a three-year low, 2020 to 2025, 580 trades.

7. At a realistic price about 58 cents came back per dollar of premium. About 88 per cent expired worthless. The same calls on ordinary stocks did about the same.

8. The test did not fail because quiet is bad. It failed because quiet was only the entry condition; the edge was the judgement of the event, and a screen cannot borrow an eye.

9. A goat is taken in passing, not hunted for a season. The trade is small, on the way, and does not change the march.

10. Most of the time the premium is the seller's. Knowing that is the buyer's first skill; knowing the exception is the second.

## Commentary

The volatility risk premium is the seller's goat. Implied volatility tends to sit above the volatility later realised; the term curve usually sits in contango; and away from a dated event the buyer of protection has little reason to be paid. A buyer who pays for calm is buying insurance in fair weather, at a price set by someone who knows the weather.

The exception has a shape, not a slogan. After a volatility crush, implied can fall below realised. If a dated event still lies ahead, the seller has left the goat untended: the premium no longer covers the risk, and the buyer feeds no one. Only then is the goat on the road at all.

The mechanical version was tested and failed. A pre-registered study bought twelve-month calls struck 30 per cent out of the money whenever a stock's realised volatility was at a three-year low, over 2020 to 2025, 580 trades. At a realistic price, about $0.58 came back per $1 of premium. About 88 per cent expired worthless. The same calls on ordinary stocks did no better; the quiet added nothing.

That clause is the whole finding. The counterweight to Mai's long-dated buying is not that long-dated buying is wrong; it is that quiet was his entry condition, not his edge. In Schwager's Hedge Fund Market Wizards, and in Lewis's The Big Short, the edge is the reading of a particular situation. A screen can find quiet. It cannot borrow the eye that knows which quiet is load-bearing.
