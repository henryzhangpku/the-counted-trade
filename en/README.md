### Part I: Stratagems for Winning

The first six chapters are the count before the battle. A stratagem is a trick played on the enemy, and the buyer's first enemy is the hope trade dressed as conviction. The five sentences are the camouflage test: say them aloud.

The buyer's whole art is here in outline: attacking the mispricing rather than the price, letting a dated catalyst do the work, setting a time stop before entry, measuring the fire before looting it, and striking where the edge actually lands.

### Part II: Stratagems for Confronting the Enemy

Chapters seven to twelve read the other side's price. A backtest can be made from nothing; the quiet road after an event can be cheaper than the event itself; most events are priced well enough that watching is a position; the far-OTM weekly sells a smile and hides a dagger; the debit spread trades a tail for a breakeven win rate; and the volatility premium usually belongs to the seller.

For the buyer, this is the discipline of refusal: knowing when the premium is the seller's, and which tree he is in.

### Part III: Stratagems for Attack

The third six are the funnel. Thousands of candidates go in; liquidity, a dated catalyst, volatility, structure, risk and edge filter them; five to ten come out, at most. Most candidates fail, and that is the funnel working.

The chapters put paper before money: minimum size, fresh data, a market the buyer can leave, a plan for scaling out, and one king at a time.

### Part IV: Stratagems for Confused Situations

Chapters nineteen to twenty-four are about risk when the field is chaos. Max loss is known before entry; premium is dearest when buying feels most urgent; the golden cicada leaves before expiry; the plan locks at entry; correlated trades are one bet; and single-stock and index options are two roads.

The buyer survives confusion by counting what can be lost, not by predicting the confusion.

### Part V: Stratagems for Gaining Ground

The fifth six are about testing without fooling yourself. A hit rate is not a win rate; a rule that was never a rule is a rotten beam; honest labels are the card; an empty board is an answer; the ladder is fixed before entry; one blossom does not make a spring; and the journal owns the process.

Most bets lose. The question is whether the few winners paid for all the rest, after costs, in a sample large enough to trust.

### Part VI: Stratagems for Desperate Situations

The last six are the drawdown. Walk-forward splits the sample; the empty fort is sometimes the strongest position; the option chain is the enemy's spy; the small loss and the kill rule end a setup; the week is a chain of routines; and retreat is the last stratagem because it is the first.

The account that leaves the field returns to it.
