# Before the Count

A stratagem is a trick played on an enemy. The Thirty-Six Stratagems do not pretend otherwise: show weakness where you are strong, offer a bait worth taking, let the enemy's own certainty do the work. A stratagem succeeds less because the enemy is foolish than because he sees what he expects to see.

In trading, the enemy most often tricked is yourself.

This is the buyer's book. *The Art of Options War* reads the seller of the defined-risk credit spread, paid to hold a line. *The Counted Trade* stands on the other side of that trade. The buyer pays a debit, and the debit is the whole risk; his upside is the asymmetry he paid for. Time is his enemy, volatility his price, and a catalyst his only ally. His art is choosing which mispricing, timing which dated event, paying the right price, and committing correctly: conviction in the rules, a staged ladder, and a size small enough that one loss is trivial.

Six sets of six. Each chapter turns one stratagem's image to the buyer's problem. Before any trade, five sentences must each be sayable in one sentence: the edge, the catalyst, the max loss, the time stop, the breakeven win rate. A trade that cannot say all five is a hope trade.

A second voice runs beside them: Jamie Mai, a buyer of cheap convexity, as told in Jack Schwager's *Hedge Fund Market Wizards* and Michael Lewis's *The Big Short*. The book also records where a mechanical copy of his method failed. The quiet was only his entry condition; the event was his edge. A screen cannot borrow his eye.

This book is education, not advice. It contains no personal recommendations, no signals and no promises. Options can lose money faster than stock. A long option can lose its entire premium, and a debit spread can lose its whole debit; the debit can still be more than you can afford. Nothing here is a performance claim.

Read one chapter a day. Then read them again in the sets' order, for the order is the argument: count before the battle, read the other side's price, pass the funnel, hold the max loss in chaos, test without fooling yourself, and know when to leave.
