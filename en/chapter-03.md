# 3. Kill With a Borrowed Knife

*Part I: Stratagems for Winning*

1. A knife is borrowed, not forged. The hand supplies the direction; another man's weight supplies the blow. The borrower's whole art is knowing where the blade will pass.

2. The buyer's knife is dated. An event on a day, a mechanism that reprices, and a chain that has not yet charged for either.

3. Therefore the sentence is: this event, on this date, should reprice the stock, because of this mechanism. A catalyst without a date is a rumour. A date without a mechanism is a coin.

4. Earnings, a rate decision, a price report, a drug decision, a court date, an index rebalance, an expiration. The calendar is short, and the knife must stand on it.

5. Every candidate goes on one calendar of forty-five days. What falls outside it is not a catalyst for this trade. It is another trade, entered early.

6. The sharpest knife is a binary one: a ruling or a regulator's decision, where the stock has two futures and the chain prices one curve.

7. In that case the buyer need not know which future arrives. He needs the two futures to be priced as one, and the knife does the rest.

8. A borrowed knife has two edges. If the date passes and the price has not moved, the event was the wrong knife, and the trade is wrong even if the story is still good.

9. Therefore: no dated mechanism, no catalyst. No catalyst, no trade. The buyer pays for time, and time without an event is rent paid to nobody.

10. The wise buyer does not swing hardest. He waits until the enemy's own weight is already moving, and holds the blade where it must pass.

## Commentary

The calendar does the discipline. Forty-five days holds a company's next report, a macro date and one legal hearing; it is short enough that the buyer is not paying for a year of hope. Every candidate goes on that single sheet, the funnel's first gate: a thesis whose event is not on it is not ready, however good the story.

The events divide into four families. Company events: earnings, guidance, an investor day, a drug decision. Macro events: the rate decision, the inflation print, the employment report. Legal and regulatory events: a ruling, an approval, an investigation. Mechanical events: an index rebalance, a large expiration, a lockup. Each has a date and each has a mechanism by which it reaches the price. Name the mechanism or the date is only a day on which something might happen.

The binary knife is sharpest because the mispricing is structural. When the outcome is yes or no, the stock has two futures, but the chain quotes a continuum. As Mai put it in Schwager's *Hedge Fund Market Wizards*, the price of that continuum can be wrong in a way that needs no forecast. The buyer supplies the date and the judgement that the event is real; the event supplies the force.
