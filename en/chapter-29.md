# 29. Deck the Tree With False Blossoms

*Part V: Stratagems for Gaining Ground*

1. Silk blossoms are tied to a bare tree. From the road it is spring; up close it is string and dye.

2. A sample can be decked the same way. One spread that returned several times its debit makes a season.

3. Look at the median, not the blossom. The middle trade is what the method usually does.

4. Look at coverage: how many candidates the rule found, how many it could fill, how many it counted.

5. Look at the whole distribution, including the worthless expiries and the trades never entered.

6. In the options pilot, one spread at +148% stood in a sample whose median was −94%. The mean was a blossom; the median was the tree.

7. Jamie Mai's practice, as he describes it in Schwager's *Hedge Fund Market Wizards* and as Lewis tells it in *The Big Short*, was built to look like this: risk one to make ten, expect most bets to lose, let the few carry the book.

8. That is a description of a distribution, not a promise about the next trade.

9. The question is never "did most win". It is whether the few paid for all the rest, after costs, in a sample large enough to trust.

10. A tree with one blossom is not in bloom. It is a tree with one blossom.

## Commentary

The pilot is the plainest warning here. One spread returned 148%, and the sample's median was a loss of 94%. Anyone shown the winner alone would call the method promising; anyone shown the median would call it broken. Both numbers are true, and the gap between them is the whole question of sample size.

The mean of such a sample can be positive while the typical trade is a near-total loss, because a single large winner carries the average. That is not a defect of the arithmetic. It is the shape of the buyer's trade, and it is the shape Mai described: many small losses, a few large gains, the book carried by the few. In Schwager's interview he presents this as a way of thinking about payoff, not as a forecast; Lewis's account tells the same story of cheap positions held against unlikely outcomes.

So the test of the method is not "did most win". It is whether the winners paid for all the losers, after costs, in a sample large enough that one blossom cannot be mistaken for spring. A tree with one blossom is not in bloom. It is a tree with one blossom.
